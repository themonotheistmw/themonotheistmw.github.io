The Burden of Certainty

When may we speak on the strength of another person’s word?

By The Monotheist

Contents

  2. How far should doubt extend?
  3. Why verification has limits
  4. Conclusion
  5. References

Suppose three pupils tell a teacher that a fourth has copied an assignment. Each gives the same account. Only when the teacher asks what each pupil saw does she discover that all three heard the story from the same classmate. The allegation may still be true. What has changed is the teacher’s reason for believing it. An apparent convergence of witnesses has become a single report whose basis she has yet to establish. How should that discovery change what she is prepared to say and do?

The question matters because the ordinary dependence of one person on another makes both learning and injustice possible. A teacher who insists on witnessing everything herself can learn little about what happens outside her classroom. A teacher who treats everything she hears as knowledge can damage a pupil’s reputation in a single sentence. I argue that the duty to inquire arises when a weakness in the evidence bears on a judgment someone is about to make. Its extent depends on the claim being advanced and on what others may suffer if it is wrong.

What a correct judgment can conceal

Edmund Gettier’s first counterexample to the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief turns on a detail easy to overlook. Smith has strong grounds to believe that Jones will get a particular job and knows that Jones carries ten coins. He therefore concludes that the successful candidate carries ten coins. In fact, Smith gets the job and happens to carry ten coins himself. His conclusion is true. Yet the coins he counted belonged to Jones. The fact that makes his conclusion true played no part in his inquiry. [1]

The classroom case has a different structure, but Gettier helps identify a temptation common to both. If the pupil later admits copying, the teacher might retrospectively claim that the three accounts confirmed one another. The admission establishes what the pupil did. It adds nothing to the independence of the original witnesses. Judging an inquiry solely by its eventual result would vindicate a method that could just as easily have condemned an innocent pupil.

How far should doubt extend?

If even a well-supported judgment can be mistaken, one might demand certainty before trusting any report. Al-Ghazālī gives that demand its most searching formulation in the opening of Deliverance from Error. He recalls how strongly a child’s religious convictions reflect the community in which the child grows up. The observation gives him a reason to ask how he acquired his own convictions. Their origin alone cannot decide which of them are true. He therefore asks what kind of cognition could withstand the possibility that one’s grounds themselves are faulty. [2]

His example of a shadow gives the inquiry its force. A glance presents the shadow as stationary. Observation over time reveals movement too gradual to register in that glance. Reason corrects the initial judgment. Al-Ghazālī then turns the correction against his own confidence in reasoning. If a higher faculty exposed the limits of sight, on what grounds could reason exclude a corresponding limit of its own? He describes his eventual recovery of trust in rational first principles as a divine illumination. A further proof would already have depended on the very principles in question. [2]

There is a lesson here about the proper object of doubt. A teacher can resolve the question of whether three pupils observed an incident by asking each how they learned of it. Requiring her first to settle the ultimate reliability of perception would leave the identifiable defect in her evidence untouched. Al-Ghazālī’s inquiry concerns the foundations on which any proof proceeds. The teacher faces a local question about a particular chain of testimony. Intellectual care requires her to identify which link actually needs examination.

Why verification has limits

The Qur’an places an obligation on the recipient of a report at the point where believing it could lead to action against others.

In his commentary on this verse, al-Ṭabarī examines two readings of the imperative, fa-tabayyanū and fa-tathabbatū. He judges their meanings close: the recipient should allow time to establish the report’s soundness before accepting it. The interval between hearing and acting is ethically significant because a judgment reached in haste can injure the people named in the report. The first point is al-Ṭabarī’s gloss. The application to a teacher hearing an allegation is mine. [4]

In the classroom, the distinction between hearing and seeing has a practical consequence. The teacher can testify to having heard the allegation. To say she knows what the pupil did would require grounds that reach beyond that conversation. A further verse places a limit on the means she might use to obtain them.

Nothing in the example requires treating a pupil as a fāsiq, the term Pickthall renders “evil-liver” in 49:6. Every pupil might have spoken sincerely. What carries over is the listener’s responsibility for the use she makes of a report. Asking who witnessed the copying bears directly on the allegation. Asking classmates to disclose the accused pupil’s private failings would enlarge the injury without resolving the question. Verse 49:12 gives that boundary an ethical basis in its treatment of suspicion, spying and backbiting.

An objection remains. A rule that requires stronger grounds for a public accusation than for a private question may appear to make truth depend on the consequences of speaking. The pupil either copied or did not. But what changes with the consequences is the action the teacher is entitled to take on her present evidence. A repeated report could warrant asking privately to see a draft or hear how an answer was reached. Announcing misconduct to the class would add a judgment of guilt that the teacher cannot yet defend. She can act promptly while keeping open the question on which the pupil deserves a hearing.

Conclusion

At the end of the first conversation, the teacher knows something quite definite. Three pupils have repeated an allegation. She can establish where it began and ask the accused pupil to account for the work. Until she obtains evidence of copying, she has grounds for those inquiries and for describing what was reported. A finding of misconduct would require a further reason. The distinction survives even if the pupil subsequently confesses.

This is the burden that accompanies certainty in public speech. Someone who says “I know” invites others to rely on a judgment and may set their actions in motion. The speaker owes them an account of how the evidence reaches the person or event in question. Doubt serves an ethical purpose when it uncovers a missing connection in time for someone to correct a judgment, hear an answer, or spare another person an undeserved accusation.
