The Moral Danger of Believing Oneself Virtuous

What do our good deeds entitle us to say about ourselves?

By The Monotheist

Contents

  3. The obligation to give evidence against oneself
  4. Conclusion
  5. References

Imagine a donor who has kept a village reading room open through three winters. At the annual meeting he notices that the treasurer has reimbursed herself for a purchase without entering a receipt. He asks that the payment be recorded as unverified until she produces it. Then another member turns back a page. The donor claimed an expense on exactly the same terms. He is indignant. Everyone in the room knows what he has done for the library. Must he also produce a receipt?

His indignation poses a harder ethical question than ordinary hypocrisy. The money he gave was his to keep. The books his neighbours read are really there. He may also be quite right about the treasurer’s account. What, then, has gone wrong? He has taken evidence of one good action as a reason to exempt another action from examination. This essay asks how that inference works, why it survives sincere religious commitment, and what a community owes both to a person who raises a justified criticism and to the person who must answer it.

What a good deed establishes

A history of generosity counts for something. If a stranger accused the donor of having contributed nothing, the reading room’s accounts would settle the matter in his favour. His neighbours might also reasonably ask him to take charge of a future appeal. Experience of his conduct bears on what they can entrust to him. Yet neither the gift nor his neighbours’ gratitude tells them whether a particular expense was properly documented. They could believe every favourable thing he says about the gift and still need the missing receipt.

The danger begins with a modest shift in the question under discussion. Asked what evidence supports the expense, the donor answers by describing the sort of man he is. The shift succeeds because everyone can see a truth in his answer, and because asking again feels ungrateful. The treasurer, meanwhile, must defend her entry on its merits. He has secured a privilege without having to propose a rule that would give it to him. Moral standing can distort a public judgment in precisely this quiet way.

Self-praise and the knowledge of character

The verse places the warning about claiming purity beside an account of what God knows of a person before that person can remember anything of himself. The sequence sets a limit on the speaker’s authority. He can remember a gift, name its recipient, and describe what it cost him. Those facts give him only a partial view of a life whose beginnings were hidden even from him. Al-Qurṭubī, commenting on the closing prohibition, glosses it as a restraint on praising oneself. He connects that restraint with avoiding ostentation and cultivating humility. [1]

Al-Ghazālī offers a further distinction in the Revival of the Religious Sciences. In his discussion of self-admiration (ʿujb) and presumptuous entitlement (idlāl), he considers a person who gives something to another. The giver can come to admire the size of his own favour. A further change occurs when he expects the recipient to perform services for him and regards refusal as a failure to give him his due. Al-Ghazālī situates this example within an account of gratitude to God. A person can rejoice in a good quality as a gift received. Self-admiration arises when he dwells on the quality as his own excellence and loses sight of its giver. [2]

The reading-room example extends that distinction into a question of public authority. The donor asks his neighbours to repay their gratitude by excusing him from a question they may ask of anyone else. His generosity could have been sincere on the day he gave. The later claim to special treatment is a fresh act with consequences of its own. This explains how the committee can honour the gift and examine the expense without inconsistency.

The obligation to give evidence against oneself

Al-Ṭabarī asks a practical question about this verse. In what sense can a witness testify against himself? His example is a person who acknowledges that someone else has a rightful claim against him. That admission gives the other person something more useful than a general profession of humility. It identifies what is owed and to whom. Al-Ṭabarī also reads the reference to rich and poor as a warning that partiality to either can corrupt testimony. [3]

The distinction matters when humility is demanded of the wrong person. Suppose the treasurer produces the second entry and an elder reproaches her for embarrassing a benefactor. She has made a claim that the members can check for themselves. Requiring silence would deprive them of evidence, and it would make her bear the entire cost of preserving his reputation. Conversely, the missing receipts establish a defect in the record. They cannot, by themselves, establish theft. The members have reason to examine both payments, give both people an opportunity to supply the evidence, and record the outcome where the original criticism was heard. These are practical judgments about this case. Their justification lies in giving everyone affected access to an accurate account.

Conclusion

What would count as moral progress for the donor? He could declare before the meeting that he, too, is an imperfect man. Everyone might applaud his modesty, while the treasurer remained the only person required to account for a payment. A more exacting response would be to leave her entry open for examination, place his own beside it, and accept the same decision if neither can be supported. If he had insinuated dishonesty in public, he would also owe her a public correction of whatever the evidence failed to establish.

The donor’s past generosity gives the village a reason to thank him. It gives him experience he might put to its service again. Its moral value becomes precarious when he spends the gratitude it earned on immunity for a different act. The person best placed to prevent that exchange is the donor himself, at the moment the ledger turns to his page.
