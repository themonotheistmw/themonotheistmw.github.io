Reason, Revelation and the Burning of Cotton

What al-Ghazālī and Ibn Rushd disputed about proof

By The Monotheist

A piece of cotton touches a flame and turns to ash. Al-Ghazālī and Ibn Rushd could agree about what an observer would ordinarily see. They disagreed about what the observer could establish from it. Does fire exercise a power that explains the burning? Could God preserve the cotton even when the conditions for burning appear complete? The distance between those questions matters. An argument sufficient to establish a cause might still fall short of proving that an exception is impossible.

Histories organized around a conflict between reason and revelation can obscure this dispute at its most interesting point. Al-Ghazālī asks what would entitle a philosopher to exclude a miracle. Ibn Rushd asks what would remain of natural knowledge if things had no powers by which their effects could be understood. Their exchange shows how much turns on the strength of a particular inference. It also explains why Ibn Rushd, when he turns to scripture, makes the authority of a figurative interpretation depend on what the interpreter has actually proved.

What the observer sees

In the seventeenth discussion of The Incoherence of the Philosophers, al-Ghazālī considers familiar pairs of events, among them drinking and the quenching of thirst. We can grasp what drinking is without including in its definition the satisfaction that ordinarily follows. Calling the connection necessary therefore requires an argument about the world. The meaning of the words alone cannot establish it. With cotton and fire, the issue becomes whether observation supplies the missing argument. [1]

Consider the opponent whom al-Ghazālī addresses first. On this account fire acts by nature. Given receptive cotton and the appropriate contact, it has no choice about producing combustion. Al-Ghazālī asks what a witness has seen that distinguishes this account from one in which God brings about the cotton’s blackening and disintegration on the occasion of contact. Both accounts accommodate the sequence the witness describes. Repeated observation can strengthen the expectation of burning while leaving open the further question of what makes it happen. [1]

The argument has a precise limit. Agreement with the observations makes divine production an available explanation for someone who already has reasons to affirm divine agency. That agreement cannot by itself establish that God produces combustion directly. Al-Ghazālī’s immediate argumentative task is narrower and easier to meet. A philosopher who calls a miracle impossible must supply a reason for excluding alternatives consistent with the ordinary course of events.

What a cause explains

Ibn Rushd answers al-Ghazālī directly in The Incoherence of the Incoherence. His reply begins from the differences among things we encounter. We recognize particular agents through the characteristic things they do. If water, fire and every other substance were equally unrelated to the effects that follow them, on what basis could we distinguish their natures? A discipline that records which events usually follow others might still support prediction. Its claim to explain why a particular substance produces a particular change would be in jeopardy. [2]

The distinction between a cause and a sufficient cause gives the reply much of its force. Acknowledging that fire plays a real part in burning leaves room to ask what else the effect requires. The material on which the fire acts matters, as does anything that impedes its action. An investigator who finds that a particular piece of cotton failed to burn has reason to examine those conditions. Indeed, the ability to ask which condition was missing presupposes that some features of the situation make a difference to the outcome. Ibn Rushd leaves open in the cited passage whether a natural cause also depends on an external cause to complete its action. [2]

Does this answer al-Ghazālī? It answers one formulation of the challenge more directly than another. Against a denial that created things have any causal powers, Ibn Rushd identifies an intellectual cost. Such a denial owes us an account of what the investigator learns when she discovers why a material burns. Against the narrower claim that observation fails to establish an absolutely exceptionless connection, the reply is less decisive. One can acknowledge the contribution of fire and still ask whether a complete description of ordinary causes exhausts the possibilities open to God. To pass from one claim to the other would require a further premise about divine action.

Al-Ghazālī himself tests an alternative in the same discussion. He allows his opponent the supposition that fire acts consistently upon similar materials, then asks whether the properties of the fire or of a prophet exposed to it could be altered. On that supposition, survival in the fire would still be conceivable. The two arguments do different work. The first disputes what ordinary observation can establish about agency. The second grants a stable causal disposition and asks whether the conditions for its exercise must always obtain. This is why a single declaration that al-Ghazālī rejected causation gives an inadequate account of the discussion’s method. [1]

When a proof bears on a reading

What gives this disagreement a place in the history of scriptural interpretation? Ibn Rushd offers an answer in the Decisive Treatise. In sections 12–14 he considers a case in which demonstration has established a conclusion about an existing thing. If revelation also speaks of it and the apparent sense of the passage differs from the demonstrated conclusion, the interpreter must consider a figurative reading. Arabic usage constrains the meanings the words can bear. The rule thus places demands on both parts of the argument. The philosophical conclusion must have the standing claimed for it, and the proposed interpretation must make linguistic sense. [3]

The Qur’anic account of Abraham supplies a concrete instance against which to test the rule.

The verse addresses the fire directly. It gives no account of which physical conditions changed. Suppose a reader nevertheless insists on a figurative interpretation because survival in the fire is physically impossible. To invoke Ibn Rushd’s rule, that reader would need to defend the premise of impossibility. The ordinary expectation of injury would be insufficient. Even with the premise established, the reader would still need to explain the proposed figurative sense of the passage. This is an application of the rule to the question raised by al-Ghazālī. It leaves open how Ibn Rushd himself would interpret any particular account of a miracle.

Conclusion

The burning cotton brings two burdens of proof into view. Al-Ghazālī shows why a familiar sequence of events gives insufficient grounds, by itself, for ruling out divine intervention. Ibn Rushd shows what a critic of natural powers must explain in return. An account of the world ought to make intelligible why differences between things matter to what they can do. Each argument retains force at the point where the other leaves a question open.

For the intellectual historian, the consequence is concrete. Calling a thinker a defender of reason tells us little until we identify the conclusion for which that thinker claims a proof. In this exchange, establishing that fire contributes to combustion, establishing that an exception is impossible, and justifying a figurative reading of scripture are three distinct achievements. The historical disagreement becomes intelligible once we can say which of them an author has undertaken and how far his reasons carry him.
